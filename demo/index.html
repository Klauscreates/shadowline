At the pole, the Sun never rises more than about 1.5°

  • Every hill throws a shadow tens of kilometres long.
  • A good site needs sunlight for power and a clear line to Earth for contact.
  • Both change hour by hour, and a few hundred metres can decide it.
  • Shadowline lets anyone see that and test any spot in one click.

Walkthrough · step 1 of 3

Pick a site

  • The app opens with three south-pole sites loaded. Add your own by clicking the map, typing coordinates, or choosing a named site.
  • Here: the IM-2 landing target on Mons Mouton. Over one lunar day it is sunlit 52.5% of the time and has Earth in view 83.1%.
  • Right now the Sun is 0.6° below the terrain, with sunrise in 3 hours. Earth is in view, with Earthset in 19.5 days.
  • An eye-level horizon view shows the same skyline with the Sun and Earth at true size.
3 / 7
Walkthrough · step 2 of 3

Change the arrival date

  • Set a mission length, here 14 days. The table shows the same mission arriving on time and 1, 3 and 7 days late.
  • On time, the mission is sunlit 97% of the time. Slip a week and that drops to 63%, with a 5-day stretch of darkness.
  • Slip only one day and it improves to 100%. Timing matters as much as place.
  • Find arrival windows searches the next 60 days and ranks the best dates. One click moves the mission there.
4 / 7
Walkthrough · step 3 of 3

Compare the options

  • Up to four sites side by side, same dates, best value in each row in green.
  • The trade-off is plain: the Shackleton ridge has sunlight 100% of the lunar day but Earth in view only 49.5%. The IM-2 target is the reverse: 52.5% sun, 83.1% Earth.
  • The Shackleton crater floor, 20 km away, gets neither. It is in permanent shadow.
  • Power, communication and ground slope stay as separate numbers, so a team can weigh them its own way.

Real NASA data, checked

JPL Horizons

Sun and Earth positions come straight from NASA JPL Horizons. The in-browser backup agrees to 0.008° (0.004° against NASA SPICE).

0.1%

Average gap between the GPU heat maps and an independent site-by-site calculation, over 60 random points.

250 m

Terrain grid, from NASA's 240 m LOLA data. Smaller rocks and ridges are not seen. "Earth in view" is line of sight, not a radio link budget.

Open data, open code, any screen

  • Phone and desktop: the same app, with touch controls, on a phone or a wide monitor.
  • Terrain: LOLA LDEM_60S_240MPP_ADJ, NASA GSFC Planetary Geodesy Data Archive.
  • Check data: NASA NAIF SPICE kernels (DE440).
  • Libraries: three.js and astronomy-engine, both MIT.
  • AI use: code written with Claude (Anthropic) as a coding assistant, directed and reviewed by the author.
  • Code: github.com/Klauscreates/shadowline
